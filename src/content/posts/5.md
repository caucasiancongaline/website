Is it cringe to write a July 4th post?

From fifteen miles away, as I sat on my couch on another annoyingly hot weekday night, I swear I could hear the kids screaming bloody murder on the roller coasters, smell the briny sea / cigarette fragrance cocktail, feel the hars hrays of the sun on my burnt, salty skin. Coney Island was calling my name. I spent most of my evening on the 3rd trying to figure out if making the trip to Nathan's Famous International Hot Dog Eating Contest was plausible. I had the day off, no plans at all, not a single care in the world. The only variable left to account for was my own willpower. 

I searched reddit for tips. I looked for youtube vlogs. I read the usa today guide. I mapped out a possible subway journey. Ultimately, after at least an hour of wrestling with myself, I decided that attending the event would have been a boondoggle I was unwilling to subject myself to. Too many people, too many logistics, too much standing around, too little action. I closed my laptop, relieved that I had made a decision, a bit dissapointed in myself for not mustering up the courage to do the fun thing. I have a habit of not doing the fun thing. I'm working on it. July 4th won't be an step in the right direction.

I woke up the next day and formulated a new plan that was still on the holiday theme, but much more my usual speed: going to the Met and viewing the American wing. Boring? Kind of. Cool? Only if you're kind of a loser or old. A good use of my day off? Debatable. But, does it tie in with the holiday theme of celebrating our nation's birthday? One hundred percent yes. Here we go.

I start the walk across the park. I put in my Pixel Buds and continued what I last left off listening to: Bill Simmons and Ryen Russillo talking about some bullshit going on in the NBA. Awesome content, but pure slop. I take my headphones off. Today is not the day for listening to dumb NBA podcasts. Today is a day that I have off from my 9-5 in celebration of our (great?) nation's birthday. I am in the most famous park in the most famous city that our country has to offer. There are people of all religions, creeds, races, nationalities, genders, and political affiliations congregating on this urban oasis, taking in the sunshine as they, too, celebrate life in this beautiful world. I don't need to be walking in the bubble my headphones create. I take in the world around me unadulterated.

I reach 5th Ave, on the East side of the park. Scattered amongst the hundreds of people is a smattering of food carts. Each kiosk dons their own sun-bleached signs, menus taped over with updated prices, and a generating loudly humming over the patrons placing their orders. I scan the menus, taking a note of the prices. $8 for a hot dog? A bit too rich for my blood. I'm not even that hungry right now. It's museum time.

I step inside the beautiful behemoth of a building and get in line to purchase a ticket. There's a line, but I know it moves fast. I read the New Yorker whilst waiting. Is it obnoxious to read the New Yorker while in line for the Met? Is it more annoying to seek assurance that it's not obnoxious? These are the questions I wrestle with as I ignore my article and inch closer to the front of the line. The cashier raises his hand to usher me to the desk. It's pay what you want admission for me, a New York resident. "How much would you like to pay?" I pause for few beats, pretending to mull over a few amounts until I decide it's an appropriate amount of time to state the amount that I had in mind when I left my apartment. "Uhh, let's do five dollars." I'm in.

I head up the stairs and make a beeline to the American wing. I walk through a couple rooms of boring furniture before I get to the main event: Emanuel Leutze's *Washington Crossing the Deleware*. I've seen it in person multiple times before, but there's always a feeling of fundamental awe when laying fresh eyes upon it. It's a gigantic piece of work, encased in a thick golden frame with an eagle perched at the very top. In a world where everything seems expendable, unimportant, ironic, and temporary, paintings like this one remind you of what art can be. It can be bombastic, epic, earnest, and quite literally larger-than-life. It's humbling to see something like this and know that I'll never be able to create something this detailed, intricate, and enduring. I linger for about five more minutes, watching the other visitors take their photos and gaze with a similar transfixation.

I duck in and around a few more rooms in the wing. Some of the paintings have an accompanying audio blurb, which I listen to in my headphones. I gaze at many of the pastoral landscapes painted by the Hudson River School. I tell myself that I must head upstate at some point this fall to see their world in my own eyes. I'm starting to get hugry. I kind of want a hot dog.

My feet start to tire. I find a relatively quiet atrium towards the back of the Met with low foot traffic, find a chair, and pick up where I left off in *Severance* by Ling Ma. I'm just a few dozen pages from the end, so I settle in, and finish all that I have remaining. Great book. Frighteningly prescient, given that it was written just a few years before COVID ever happened. Bad times. I feel accomplished and quite hungry. It's time to leave.
